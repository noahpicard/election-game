Write yourself into the race. Take positions on the five issues that happen to define this cycle. Buy the airwaves, work the doors, court the money, and find out what fifty-one separate electorates actually think of you.

Everything runs locally in this browser. No accounts, no server, no real people.

How it works

The electorate

Every state gets 100 simulated voters. Each one is given an age band and a gender drawn from that state's real-ish demographic mix. Those three things — state, age, gender — are your three voter dimensions.

Each state, each age band and each gender has its own opinion on each of the five issues, scored −3 to +3. A voter's own position is the average of their three, plus a little personal noise.

Older voters turn out more reliably than younger ones, so a vote won among the over-65s is worth more actual ballots than one won among the under-35s.

How a vote is decided

A voter's vector is their five issue scores followed by their bias toward each candidate (everyone starts at zero; advertising moves it).

A candidate's vector is their five stances followed by +3 in their own slot and −3 in every rival's.

The voter picks whichever candidate gives the highest cosine similarity. That is the whole model — issue alignment plus accumulated goodwill.

Cosine compares direction, not distance. A candidate who holds no position on anything points nowhere and is nobody's best match. Conviction in the wrong direction loses too — but a blank platform loses to everything.

The campaign

You get money each round. Television reaches old voters, the internet reaches young ones, print reaches almost nobody but never blows up in your face. Visits and field work hit one state hard.

Everything has diminishing returns — the fourth identical buy does roughly a third of what the first one did — and everything can backfire. You are never told the exact number you moved; you are told what happened, the way a campaign manager would tell you.

Between rounds you get a poll: your true standing, blurred by gaussian noise. On election night the real numbers arrive, jittered one last time.
